You Keep the Economy Moving. Where Does Your Share Go?

← Back to the journal

Most people don't wake up in the morning thinking about the economy.

They wake up thinking about getting to work, getting the children ready, paying the bills, filling the car, buying food, and somehow finding enough time to do it all again tomorrow.

And yet, whether they think about it or not, they're keeping an enormous economic machine moving.

Every purchase sets something in motion. A shop pays a supplier. The supplier pays its people. A delivery company buys fuel. Someone pays rent on a warehouse. Taxes are collected. Equipment is repaired. Another order is placed.

The money doesn't simply disappear when it leaves your account. It changes hands. And what happens after that matters.

I want to ask a question that sounds simple but deserves a serious answer: if ordinary people help keep this machine running, where does their share of its success go?

First, we need to be fair about what happens to the money.

When you spend £100 at a business, that business has not made £100 in profit. It may have to pay for the goods it sold, the people who served you, the building, electricity, insurance, delivery, maintenance, taxes and the cost of replacing what it used.

Some businesses operate on narrow margins. Some lose money. Others make substantial profits, but those profits can also fund investment, absorb risk, or keep the business alive when conditions turn against it.

That distinction matters. I don't want to build UNIO on a misleading picture of how commerce works.

But once a business has paid its genuine costs, looked after its obligations and set aside what it reasonably needs to remain strong, another question appears.

Who should benefit from what remains?

The familiar answer is that profits belong to the owners, according to the company's legal and financial arrangements. Investors supplied capital. Founders took risks. People who made decisions should be rewarded for making good ones.

I can understand that argument. I don't dismiss it. Without investment, organisation and risk-taking, many businesses would never exist.

But I don't think that is the only arrangement we are capable of designing.

Consider the customer. Nobody forced a business to exist for their benefit alone. But without customers choosing it, again and again, it cannot keep trading. Consider the worker, whose time and effort turn an idea into a service someone can actually use.

Neither contribution automatically creates a legal entitlement to profits. I'm not claiming that it does.

I'm asking whether we could deliberately build a business in which those contributions are recognised in the rules from the beginning.

Economists measure household spending because it is a real part of economic activity. The UK's Office for National Statistics records household consumption alongside production, income and investment in the national accounts. It is one of the ways we understand how the economy functions.

That doesn't mean every pound spent becomes a pound of new wealth, or that every customer is responsible for every pound of profit. It means our everyday choices are part of a wider system of transactions, costs, work and returns.

And that system is not a law of nature. The way a company allocates its remaining profit is a design decision, within the limits of the law and its obligations.

This is where UNIO comes in.

I am not asking you to spend more money, to abandon the businesses you already know, or to pretend that buying something is an act of charity.

I'm asking whether the next business you choose could offer good value and a different destination for its success.

The intention is to create a commercial business that pays its bills, compensates people for their work, invests in its future, and then distributes the profit genuinely available for distribution to the people who helped make it successful.

Customers would participate through their qualifying activity. Workers would participate through their contribution of time. The founder would not simply treat the surplus as a personal withdrawal account.

Those are principles, not proof that the finished system works. We still have to establish the accounting, legal protections, safeguards and operating economics that make the promise sustainable and independently verifiable.

Nor would a profit share replace fair wages, reliable service or responsible prices. A business cannot excuse poor treatment today with a vague promise of a reward tomorrow.

I think that point is especially important. If UNIO only works by charging people too much, paying people too little, or hiding its costs, then it has missed its purpose.

What I want is a system that earns its place in people's lives by being useful, and then handles the value left over with a greater sense of shared responsibility.

Some people will disagree. They may argue that customers already receive the product they paid for, and workers already receive their wages. That's a reasonable starting point.

My response is not that every existing company is immoral. It's that we are free to attempt another model, and to see whether it can stand on its own feet.

We don't need everybody to agree with the philosophy before we test the economics.

We need to build something people actually want, show clearly how the money moves, and let the results speak for themselves.

So the next time you buy your groceries, fill your car or pay for a service, I won't ask you to feel guilty about it.

I'll ask you to remember that your participation has value.

And that the way a business chooses to recognise that value is not beyond our ability to change.
