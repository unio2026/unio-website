I Don't Hate Profit. I Hate What We've Allowed It to Become.

← Back to the journal

I don't hate businesses.

I don't hate successful people. And I certainly don't believe that making money is something to be ashamed of.

Quite the opposite.

I believe that a business which creates something useful, serves people well, provides employment and manages its resources responsibly deserves to succeed.

But somewhere along the way, I believe we've confused success with accumulation.

We've built an economy in which some corporations can generate extraordinary profits, distribute enormous sums to their shareholders, and still have employees struggling to afford the basic necessities of life.

And somehow, we've learned to accept that as perfectly normal.

I find that difficult to reconcile.

Not because I believe everybody should earn the same amount. Not because I think ambition is wrong. And not because I expect businesses to operate as charities.

But because I believe there is a fundamental difference between rewarding success and extracting as much as possible from the people who make that success possible.

Think about it.

A business needs customers. It needs people willing to spend their hard-earned money on whatever it offers.

It needs workers. People who give their time, their energy, their skills, and sometimes a considerable part of their lives to keep that business operating.

Without those people, what exactly is the business worth?

Of course, investment matters. Risk matters. Leadership matters. Innovation matters.

I'm not suggesting otherwise.

But why have we accepted an arrangement in which the financial rewards can become increasingly concentrated, while the people responsible for generating them have so little claim to the prosperity they've helped create?

I don't believe it has to be that way.

And that's why I'm building UNIO.

Not to destroy capitalism. Not to wage war against corporations. Not to tell people where they should or shouldn't spend their money.

I'm interested in something considerably simpler.

What if we built a business that treated the value created by its customers and workers as something they should participate in, rather than something to be extracted from them?

A business that pays its expenses, compensates its workforce, invests in its future, and then shares its distributable profits with the people who helped make those profits possible.

Not as an occasional gesture of generosity.

As part of the way the business is designed to operate.

That's the idea behind UNIO.

I don't pretend to have solved every problem. There are financial, legal and operational questions that must be answered. And a good idea is worth very little if it cannot survive the realities of running a business.

But I believe the question is worth asking.

Because the answer doesn't necessarily require people to change how they live.

You already participate in the economy every day.

You buy things. You use services. You contribute your time and your money to keeping businesses alive.

I'm not asking you to stop.

I'm asking you to consider what happens to the value you create after you've contributed it.

And whether a business could be designed to handle that value differently.

I believe it can.

That's what I intend to find out.

And rather than asking you to take my word for it, I intend to document the journey, the decisions, the difficulties, and the results.

If it works, you'll see how.

If it doesn't, you'll see why.

Because ultimately, I don't want UNIO to succeed simply because people believe in me.

I want it to succeed because the system itself proves worthy of their trust.

If the business exists because of you, it should exist for you too.

That's UNIO.

Built for You. Built With You.

— The Founder
