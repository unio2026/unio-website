What If a Business Was Designed Not to Extract Wealth?

← Back to the journal

Imagine two shops on the same street. They sell the same products, charge the same prices and employ the same number of people. From the pavement, you might not notice any difference between them.

Now imagine that both have had a successful year. One is designed to reward its owners with the remaining profit. The other is designed to share the available surplus with the customers and workers who helped generate it.

Nothing about the second shop requires it to be less ambitious, less efficient or less professional. The difference is what it is built to do with its success.

That is the distinction I want to make with UNIO. But a different destination for the money is only the beginning. We also need a different set of rules.

Start with a business, not a promise

If we open a shop and nobody wants to buy what we sell, there is nothing to share. If we cannot pay our suppliers or offer a reliable service, our philosophy will not keep the doors open.

I want UNIO to compete on the merits of what it offers. A customer should be able to walk away because another business offers better value. That freedom is a useful discipline. It forces us to improve instead of relying on people's loyalty to an idea.

The first responsibility of the business is to be useful. The second is to remain financially sound. Only then can we talk seriously about sharing its success.

What is actually available?

Imagine, purely as an illustration, that a business has £100,000 in revenue excluding VAT, where applicable. Its costs and obligations before participation rewards amount to £85,000. It needs to retain a further £5,000 to protect its operations. That leaves a potential participation pool of £10,000, assuming the accounting, tax and cash-flow checks support that figure.

Those numbers are not a forecast. They are a way of showing the order in which decisions should be made. A reserve is not automatically an accounting expense, and money in the bank is not necessarily profit.

Companies House guidance distinguishes the profit and loss account, which records financial performance, from the balance sheet, which records the company's financial position. I want our participation calculations to be reconciled with proper accounts, not based on a convenient number chosen by management.

If there is no safely available surplus, there should be no promise of a payment from it. I would rather explain a disappointing year than jeopardise the business to make a headline look good.

How participation could work

My intended model divides the available participation pool equally between qualifying customers and workers. Within the customer allocation, the share would reflect each person's qualifying spending. Within the workforce allocation, it would reflect the hours contributed.

Using the illustrative pool above, each group would have £5,000 to allocate. A customer responsible for one per cent of qualifying customer activity would receive £50 from that allocation. A worker responsible for one per cent of eligible workforce hours would receive £50 from the other.

The point is not the amount. The point is that the calculation should follow a rule that people can understand before they participate. It should not depend on whether I happen to feel generous at the end of the year.

Wages would remain separate. A person's ordinary pay recognises the work they do; the participation reward recognises their contribution to the collective result. One hour would count as one unit in that reward calculation, regardless of the person's position.

Ownership is a responsibility

I intend to retain ownership of UNIO, but not the right to treat its surplus as my personal income. If I perform genuine work, I should be able to receive reasonable compensation for it. The same principle should apply to anyone doing that work.

There is an obvious weakness in this statement: what stops an owner from simply paying themselves an excessive salary, awarding a contract to a related business or keeping unnecessary reserves?

That is precisely why a promise is insufficient. I want clear compensation policies, documented decisions about reserves, scrutiny of related-party transactions and independent review of the participation calculation. The safeguards must apply to me as much as to anyone else.

Companies House already explains that directors have responsibilities concerning company rules, conflicts of interest and financial records. UNIO's intended safeguards would need to operate alongside those duties, not replace them.

A system people can question

I would like participants to be able to see how the pool was calculated, understand their own allocation and challenge an error without having to expose another person's private information.

That means records of eligible activity, a transparent calculation method, a way to correct mistakes and a published explanation of the results. It also means deciding in advance how refunds, disputed transactions and corrections are handled.

None of that should be left until the money is ready to be distributed. The rules should exist throughout the accounting period, with appropriate legal and accounting review before implementation.

We still have work to do. The tax treatment, contractual rights, independent verification and protections against future changes need to be established properly. I am describing the system I intend to build, not claiming that these rights or payments are already in operation.

But I believe the standard is clear. If the business grows, the people who make it grow should have a meaningful way to participate in that success. And if the founder changes their mind, the system should not simply bend to accommodate them.

A business with a purpose must be able to survive commercially. A business with principles must be able to survive the person who founded it.

That is what I want UNIO to become.

— The Founder
